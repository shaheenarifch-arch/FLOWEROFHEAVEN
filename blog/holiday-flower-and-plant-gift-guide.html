Holiday Flower and Plant Gifts: How to Choose, Send and Make Them Last

By Shaheen Arif · Published 2026-11-22

The best holiday flower gift is the one that fits the person's home and routine. A fresh bouquet is right for a host or a one-day celebration, a potted plant suits someone who enjoys a little care over the winter, and botanical art or reusable greenery is the safest choice for someone who travels, has pets or simply does not want anything to water.

Quick answer: for a December gift, pick fresh flowers when you want instant impact for a party or dinner, a potted plant (such as a Christmas cactus, moth orchid or amaryllis bulb) when you want something that lasts for weeks, and everlasting decor (botanical prints, metal leaf art or an artificial swag) when the person is away a lot, has curious pets or does not have time for plant care. Whatever you choose, check for pets in the home, protect the gift from the cold on the way, and give it ready to display.

Why flowers and plants make good holiday gifts

Flowers and plants sit neatly between gifts people use up and gifts people keep. A bouquet brightens a room for a week or more, while a potted plant or a piece of botanical decor can stay in the home for years. Winter adds another reason: days are short, gardens are bare in much of the country, and something green or blooming on a windowsill is a welcome change.

The trick is matching the type of gift to the person. A bouquet given to someone leaving on a trip the next morning ends up in the trash, and a fussy plant given to someone with no bright window feels like a chore. The rest of this guide is about avoiding those mismatches.

None of these is better than the others. They simply solve different problems. Many people end up mixing them: flowers for the dinner host, a plant for a parent, and a piece of art or a door swag for the friend who just moved into a new place.

Fresh-cut bouquets: the classic holiday flower gift

A bouquet is the most immediate way to say thank you or happy holidays, and it asks no long-term commitment from the person receiving it. That is why bouquets remain the default for dinner hosts, parties and hospital visits.

When a bouquet is the right choice

  • You are visiting someone's home for a meal or party. Flowers are a traditional host gift and do not need to fit anyone's taste in decor for longer than a week or two.
  • You live far away. A florist delivery is one of the few gifts that can arrive fresh, arranged and hand-delivered on a specific day.
  • Comfort matters more than longevity. For someone who is grieving or unwell, flowers are a gentle gesture that asks nothing of them.

Choosing flowers for a holiday table

Classic winter colors are deep red, white, cream, burgundy and green, often mixed with evergreen sprigs, berries or pine cones. Soft pinks and peaches are a warm, less seasonal alternative, and white with green is hard to get wrong.

Think about where the flowers will sit. For a dinner table, a low arrangement lets guests see each other across the table. Strongly scented flowers can compete with food, so many hosts prefer milder blooms in the dining room and save heavily fragrant ones for an entry or living room.

A note on lilies and pets

Lilies are popular in winter bouquets, but true lilies and daylilies are extremely dangerous to cats. Even small amounts of pollen, leaves or vase water can cause serious kidney damage. If the person has a cat, or you do not know, ask the florist to leave lilies out. It is one of the most useful questions you can ask before ordering flowers for anyone.

How to make holiday flowers last longer

A bouquet given at a party on Saturday should still look good on Wednesday. Most of what keeps cut flowers fresh is simple, and you can share these tips on a small card with your gift or use them yourself.

  1. Start with a clean vase. Bacteria left in a vase clog stems and shorten vase life. Wash it with hot, soapy water and rinse well.
  2. Recut the stems. Trim about an inch off each stem at an angle with sharp scissors or a knife before the flowers go into water. A fresh cut helps the stems drink.
  3. Strip leaves below the waterline. Leaves sitting in water rot quickly and cloud the water.
  4. Use the flower food. If the bouquet came with a packet, mix it into the water as directed. It is there for a reason.
  5. Change the water every couple of days. Top up daily, and replace it completely every two days or so, giving the stems a small trim each time.
  6. Keep flowers cool. Heating vents, radiators, fireplaces, sunny windows and the top of the television all speed up wilting. A cooler spot overnight helps.
  7. Keep them away from the fruit bowl. Ripening fruit gives off ethylene gas, which can make some flowers age faster.
  8. Remove fading blooms. Pull out tired stems as they go so the rest of the arrangement stays fresh, and rearrange into a smaller vase as the bouquet shrinks.

Potted plants that make good winter gifts

A potted plant keeps giving long after the holiday is over. The best gift plants are forgiving, look good the day they arrive and do not need expert care. Here are some reliable options, with the honest pros and cons of each.

Christmas cactus

The Christmas cactus is a favorite holiday gift because it blooms around the end of the year and can live for many years, sometimes passed down through families. It does not want desert conditions: it likes bright, indirect light and soil that is allowed to dry a little between waterings. It is also generally listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs, which makes it a good choice for pet owners. Buds can drop if it is moved around a lot while blooming.

Poinsettia

Nothing says December quite like a poinsettia. The colorful parts are actually bracts (modified leaves), and the plant stays showy for weeks if it is kept warm and out of drafts. Poinsettias dislike cold, so protect them on the way home and keep them away from drafty doors. Remove or punch holes in the decorative foil sleeve so water can drain instead of pooling around the roots. The milky sap can irritate pets' mouths and stomachs if chewed, but poinsettias are generally considered only mildly toxic rather than dangerous. Even so, place them out of reach of curious animals.

Amaryllis bulb

An amaryllis bulb is a gift with a bit of suspense. Potted bulbs send up tall stems and large trumpet-shaped flowers, usually several weeks after they start growing, so a bulb given in late November or early December may bloom in the new year. That makes it a nice choice for someone who enjoys watching something grow. Give it a bright window and water sparingly until growth starts. Amaryllis bulbs are toxic to pets, so it is not the right gift for a home with a dog that chews.

Moth orchid

Moth orchids (Phalaenopsis) are elegant, widely available and known for flowers that can last for weeks or even months. They prefer bright, indirect light and are more often killed by overwatering than underwatering. A common approach is to water thoroughly when the potting mix is nearly dry and let the pot drain completely. They are a good gift for someone with a bright bathroom or kitchen windowsill.

Easy foliage plants

Not every gift plant needs flowers. A pothos, snake plant, ZZ plant or small fern is often a better choice for a first-time plant owner because these plants tolerate a range of light and occasional missed waterings. Check pet safety before buying, as several common houseplants, including pothos and snake plant, can irritate pets if eaten. A foliage plant in an attractive basket looks finished and will outlast every bouquet on this list.

Presenting a plant gift: covers, wrapping and winter transport

Most potted plants come in plain plastic nursery pots, which are good for the plant because they drain but do not look like much of a gift. The easiest upgrade is to drop the nursery pot into a decorative cover such as a woven basket. The plant keeps its drainage, the gift looks finished, and repotting can wait until spring.

Wrapping a living gift

  • Wrap the pot, not the plant. Paper or fabric around the outside of the pot or basket looks festive and does not crush leaves or trap moisture.
  • Leave the top open. Plants need air. If you use a paper sleeve for carrying, take it off once you arrive.
  • Add a care card. A few lines on light, watering and pet safety are more useful than a long message. Mention the plant's name so they can look it up later.

Protecting plants from the cold

Cold is the biggest risk for plant gifts in winter. Many popular holiday plants, poinsettias especially, can be damaged by even a short walk from the store to the car on a freezing day. A few simple steps help:

  • Ask the store to sleeve or bag the plant, and cover it fully for the trip outside.
  • Warm up the car before you put the plant in, and make the plant the last stop on your errands.
  • Never leave a plant in a cold car for long, even if it is wrapped.
  • When you arrive, unwrap it promptly so it is not sitting in a damp sleeve, and keep it away from the front door's cold drafts.

For a recipient far away in a cold place, a locally delivered bouquet or a non-living botanical gift is usually safer than shipping a live plant.

Gifts that never wilt: botanical art and reusable greenery

Some of the most thoughtful botanical gifts are not alive at all. Prints, leaf sculptures and artificial greenery bring the look of plants into a home with none of the care, which suits travelers, pet owners, renters in dark apartments and anyone who says they kill every plant they own.

Holiday swags and artificial greenery

Fresh evergreen garlands and wreaths are lovely, but they dry out in heated homes and drop needles. Artificial greenery looks festive the day it arrives and can come out again next year. A teardrop swag is a good gift for a host or a new homeowner because it hangs on a door, wall, railing, window or mantel and does not need a stand or a table. Dried and preserved flowers

Dried and preserved flowers sit between fresh bouquets and decor. Dried flowers are air-dried and become papery; preserved flowers are treated so they stay softer for longer. Both need no water. Keep them out of direct sun, which fades color, and away from steamy bathrooms.

Matching the gift to the person

If you are still unsure, start with the person rather than the plant. These pairings are a good place to begin:

  • Dinner or party host: a bouquet that is already arranged, ideally in its own vase or in a paper wrap they can drop straight into water, so they do not have to stop hosting to find a vase. A reusable door swag also works well as a host gift.
  • Parent or grandparent: a Christmas cactus or moth orchid, which blooms in winter and can stay in the family for years.
  • Friend who just moved: a set of botanical prints or a foliage plant in a basket to help a new space feel like home.
  • Busy professional or frequent traveler: botanical art, metal leaf decor or preserved flowers. Nothing to water while they are away.
  • Home with cats or dogs: a pet-safe plant such as a Christmas cactus or orchid, or something non-living. Skip lilies, amaryllis and holly.
  • Keen gardener: an amaryllis bulb they can watch grow into bloom.
  • Someone who is grieving: a gentle bouquet in soft colors, delivered by a local florist, or a long-lasting plant they can keep as a reminder.

Holiday flower and plant gift checklist

Run through this list before you order or head to the store:

  • Does the person have cats, dogs or small children who might chew leaves or flowers?
  • Will they be home over the next week, or traveling for the holidays?
  • Do they have a bright window, or is their home mostly dim?
  • Do they like strong fragrance, or would a scent-free gift be safer?
  • Is the delivery date before the florist's holiday cut-off, and is it a day the service delivers?
  • Will a living plant be exposed to freezing temperatures on the way?
  • Does the gift arrive ready to display, in a vase, basket or cover?

Frequently asked questions

What is a good flower gift for the holidays?

A fresh bouquet in red, white or green tones is a classic choice for hosts and parties. For something longer lasting, a Christmas cactus, moth orchid or amaryllis bulb blooms in winter, and botanical prints or an artificial swag last for years with no care.

Which holiday plants are safe for cats and dogs?

Christmas cactus and moth orchids are generally listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs. Lilies are very dangerous to cats, and amaryllis, holly and mistletoe are toxic to pets. Poinsettias are considered mildly toxic and can upset the stomach. When in doubt, choose a non-living botanical gift.

How do I keep a holiday bouquet fresh longer?

Use a clean vase, recut the stems at an angle, remove leaves below the waterline, add the flower food and change the water every couple of days. Keep the flowers away from heaters, sunny windows and ripening fruit.

How far ahead should I order flowers for Christmas delivery?

Order a few days ahead if you can, because florists are busiest right before Christmas and holiday cut-offs can be earlier than usual. Through our send flowers page, same-day orders must be placed before 1 PM in the recipient's time zone, and there is no Sunday delivery.

How do I protect a potted plant from the cold on the way home?

Have the plant sleeved or bagged, warm up the car first, make it your last stop and never leave it in a cold car. Unwrap it when you get home and keep it away from drafty doors.

What is a good plant gift for someone who kills plants?

Try a forgiving foliage plant such as a snake plant or ZZ plant, or skip living plants altogether with botanical wall art, metal leaf decor, preserved flowers or reusable artificial greenery.
